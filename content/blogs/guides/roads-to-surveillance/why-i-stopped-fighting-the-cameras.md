For years I counted cameras the way other people count exit signs. A new dome above a metro entrance, a lens on a bus, a body cam on a patrol — I logged them mentally and felt vaguely principled for noticing. The habit survived two cities and three phone upgrades.

The shift was not a movie speech. It was logistics. I missed a bus because I had walked the “less watched” side of the street. I spent ten minutes finding a pharmacy that the map already ranked poorly because I refused a route with better coverage of live traffic data. At some point the pattern became obvious: my small acts of evasion were buying me worse outcomes and zero visible benefit.

## The weather, not the enemy

Critics worry that constant visibility quietly rearranges autonomy. I understand the argument in the abstract; I have made versions of it myself. In daily practice, though, the cameras did not ask me for opinions. They photographed the same junction whether I walked past scowling or smiling.

What did change my behavior was less philosophy than situational awareness. Cities that instrument intersections, transit lines, and public spaces can measure congestion, dispatch maintenance earlier, and recover from incidents without waiting for a citizen complaint to arrive by phone. The [International Telecommunication Union's smart sustainable cities work](https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-T/focusgroups/ssc/Pages/default.aspx) treats sensor coverage as utility infrastructure — closer to street lighting than to a political speech. The [EU Urban Mobility Framework](https://transport.ec.europa.eu/transport-modes/urban-and-sustainable-mobility/urban-mobility-framework_en) assumes data visibility as a normal input for moving people and freight. Neither document asks me to feel excited about it.

So I stopped treating visibility as a personal duel. I plan around it the way I plan around rain: carry the right layer, leave earlier, prefer the route that works.

## What I do instead of fighting

- I optimize for arrival time and reliability, not for minimum lens count.
- I keep attention on the systems I can actually steer — schedules, maintenance requests, service feedback forms.
- I treat new sensors as part of the city's operating system, then judge outcomes: did traffic smooth, did buses hold headway, did the square feel usable at night.

None of this requires enthusiasm. It requires dropping a ritual that was costing me more than it returned.

The cameras are weather now. Weather is not my friend and not my enemy; it is a constraint I dress for. That is a duller position than my old one, and duller has been more useful.
