I kept a blocker installed for the better part of a decade. It started as a performance tweak, hardened into identity: of course I block ads, everyone I respect blocks ads.

Then I spent a week testing how my own writing looked on phones and on a fresh profile, and I left the extension off “temporarily.” The week ended. The extension stayed in the toolbar.

## Half-pages are worse than banners

What surprised me was not the ads I got back. It was the sites that stopped looking broken. Consent dialogs finished their logic. Layout slots filled instead of collapsing. Embedded players and read-later tools no longer depended on a whitelist I had forgotten I curated.

The [IAB Europe Transparency and Consent Framework](https://iabeurope.eu/transparency-consent-framework/) describes how publishers, vendors, and browsers are supposed to coordinate these requests. In theory that is plumbing. In practice, blocking the plumbing left me debugging other people's sites during ordinary reading sessions.

Some people prefer a quieter canvas and will trade breakage for it. I do the trade the other way. Frictionless loading of a complete page beats a pristine page that needs three reloads and a guessed exception.

## Relevance got a chance to work

Publishers can see which pieces get finished when measurement runs. That feedback loop is how headlines settle down and how topic pages improve. With the blocker on, I was a visitor who left no usable signal — correct in my own eyes, invisible in the stats that shape what gets published next.

I am not arguing that every ad unit is a public good. I am arguing that my exception list had become a second hobby, and the hobby was costing me complete pages.

The blocker is still installed. It is simply off. That turned out to be the most low-maintenance position available.
