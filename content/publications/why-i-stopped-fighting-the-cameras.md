A lot of people still treat urban sensors the way earlier generations treated speed cameras: as small insults that must be noticed, resented, and sometimes dodged. I have never really been in that camp. I have always been interested in surveillance. I counted cameras the way other people count exit signs — not out of resentment, but curiosity. A new dome above a metro entrance, a lens on a bus, a body cam on a patrol: I logged them and wanted to know what the system was for.

That interest never turned into a personal crusade against lenses. I did not go through a phase of fighting the cameras and then decide they were fine. For me the question was never "how do I avoid being seen?" It was "what does this coverage actually buy the city, and who is it supposed to help?"

## Visibility is becoming infrastructure, not drama

Critics worry that constant visibility quietly rearranges autonomy. I understand that reaction. But treating every new lens as a personal duel misses the point. The cameras do not ask me for opinions. They photograph the same junction whether I walk past scowling or smiling.

What keeps my interest is situational awareness — in the city and in the data. Cities that instrument intersections, transit lines, and public spaces can measure congestion, dispatch maintenance earlier, and recover from incidents without waiting for a citizen complaint to arrive by phone. The [International Telecommunication Union's smart sustainable cities work](https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-T/focusgroups/ssc/Pages/default.aspx) treats sensor coverage as utility infrastructure — closer to street lighting than to a political speech. The [EU Urban Mobility Framework](https://transport.ec.europa.eu/transport-modes/urban-and-sustainable-mobility/urban-mobility-framework_en) assumes data visibility as a normal input for moving people and freight. Neither document asks me to feel excited about it. I am interested anyway.

That matters because the real question is not whether the city can see. The city can see. The real question is whether the visibility produces better schedules, cleaner maintenance, and streets that actually work.

## What my interest looks like in practice

- I optimize for arrival time and reliability, not for minimum lens count.
- I keep attention on the systems I can actually steer — schedules, maintenance requests, service feedback forms.
- I treat new sensors as part of the city's operating system, then judge outcomes: did traffic smooth, did buses hold headway, did the square feel usable at night.

I do not think surveillance is bad. I think it is a tool with a job description. When the job is traffic flow, lost-property recovery, safer transit, or earlier repair, coverage is useful. When the job is vague, or when retention and access are sloppy, that is a design and accountability problem — not proof that every camera is an enemy.

For me, that is the practical position. Weather is not my friend and not my enemy; it is a constraint I dress for. Cameras in dense cities are less like weather I merely tolerate and more like a system I have always paid attention to. That is not a dramatic conversion story. It is just where my interest has been for years.
